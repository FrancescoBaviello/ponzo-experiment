Comparing line lengths

In this study you compare the lengths of two red bars shown on different backgrounds. It takes about 25 minutes and must be done on a laptop or desktop computer with a keyboard, in one sitting, somewhere you won't be interrupted.

What you will do

  • Answer a few short questions about yourself.
  • Set up your screen: measure it with a bank card and do a one-minute test that estimates how far you sit from the screen.
  • Judge and adjust bar lengths on about 130 screens, with short breaks.

Your data

Taking part is voluntary: you can stop at any time by closing the page, without giving a reason. At the end you get your code; quote it if you later want your data deleted.

How did you measure?

How far are you from the screen?

Sit comfortably at about arm's length from the screen, in the position you will keep for the whole study. This test uses your eye's natural blind spot to measure the distance. It takes about a minute.

  1. Put a finger of your left hand on the Space bar.
  2. Cover your right eye with your right hand.
  3. With your left eye, keep looking at the black square on the right. Don't follow the ball.
  4. The red ball moves to the left. Press Space the moment it disappears.

What to do

Each screen shows two horizontal red bars, one above the other. Only the lower bar can change.

  1. Say whether the two bars look the same length: press S for same or D for different.
  2. Then make the lower bar look exactly as long as the upper bar: ← makes it shorter, → makes it longer (hold the key to move faster). If the bars already look equal, leave them.
  3. Press Enter when the two bars look equal.

Go by how the bars look to you: there is no time limit and no right or wrong answer. Keep your head at the same distance as in the test, use both eyes, and don't measure with your fingers or any object.

What this study was about

On many screens the upper bar looked longer than the lower one even when both had the same length. This is the Ponzo illusion: lines that converge towards a horizon make the upper bar seem farther away, and the brain enlarges what it believes is far. We tested which background features (converging lines, a horizon, shading, texture, trees) make the illusion stronger, and whether they add up.
